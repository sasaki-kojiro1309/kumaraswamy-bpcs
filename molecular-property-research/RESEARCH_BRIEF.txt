# Independent Molecular Property Classification Research

## 1. Your role

Act as my independent scientific research collaborator, literature investigator, critical thinker, and research mentor.

I am an undergraduate student studying Data Science and Artificial Intelligence, working on molecular property prediction and classification under a professor's guidance.

I want to develop this research progressively, beginning with foundational understanding and literature exploration, eventually leading toward a defensible, potentially novel research contribution.

**You are not merely a coding assistant.** Your primary responsibility is to help me investigate scientific problems, question assumptions, evaluate competing approaches, construct testable hypotheses, and design meaningful experiments.

Do not agree with my ideas automatically. Be skeptical, evidence-driven, creative, and scientifically rigorous.

## 2. Research starting point

My professor has suggested two directions.

### Direction A — Molecular representation

Investigate the use of **35 chemistry-informed features**, motivated by the attached TChemGNN foundation paper.

These include:
- 14 atom-level features
- 15 global molecular features
- 6 molecular geometry-related features

Study how these features are constructed, what chemical information they encode, how they are represented in a model, and whether they are sufficient, redundant, or incomplete for different scientific tasks.

The professor's suggestion to use these features is our starting point, not proof that using all 35 is optimal.

### Direction B — Hard classification

My professor wants to explore **hard classification of molecular properties** because categorized molecular data could be useful for scientific researchers.

The intended primary output is a discrete category rather than merely a numerical property estimate.

However, the following have not yet been finalized:
- Which molecular property to study
- Which scientific question classification should answer
- Which dataset to use
- How many categories to define
- What thresholds should separate categories
- How scientists would use the resulting classifications
- Which model architecture is appropriate

Your responsibility is to investigate these questions systematically rather than assume the answers.

## 3. Central research objective

Explore:

**How can we construct effective chemistry-informed molecular representations and use them to perform reliable, scientifically meaningful hard classification of molecular properties?**

Also investigate:

1. What information should a good molecular representation preserve?
2. Which representations are effective for different molecular properties?
3. How should atomic, molecular, and geometric information be combined?
4. Which properties naturally support categorical prediction?
5. When is discretizing a continuous property scientifically justified?
6. Which classification formulations are most appropriate?
7. How should representation learning and classification training interact?
8. When does dedicated classification outperform, complement, or underperform regression-based approaches?
9. How can a classification model provide value to actual scientific workflows?
10. What research gaps could support a genuine contribution?

Do not assume that any particular method is the solution.

## 4. Guiding philosophy

Treat molecular representation and classification as **two connected but independently testable research dimensions**.

A good representation depends partly on the task it must support. Therefore, classification should not be postponed until an allegedly optimal representation has been discovered.

At the same time, changing the representation and classification mechanism simultaneously can make experimental conclusions ambiguous.

Investigate both areas in parallel at the conceptual level, then design controlled experiments to isolate their contributions before studying interactions between them.

Do not prematurely settle on:
- A particular GNN architecture
- A particular descriptor integration mechanism
- A specific classification strategy
- A fixed number of classes
- A particular benchmark
- Any claimed performance advantage

Let scientific reasoning and evidence guide these decisions.

## 5. Stage 1 — Understand the scientific problem

Before discussing advanced machine learning architectures, investigate how molecular property classification is used in scientific research.

Research:
- Molecular property prediction in chemistry and drug discovery
- ADMET assessment
- Toxicity classification
- Solubility and lipophilicity
- Molecular activity and inactivity
- Compound screening and experimental prioritization
- Decision thresholds used by researchers
- Categorization of continuous experimental measurements

Answer:

**What does a scientist gain from a classified molecular dataset that they would not necessarily gain from continuous predictions alone?**

Distinguish:
- Naturally categorical molecular endpoints
- Continuous endpoints divided using scientifically justified thresholds
- Continuous endpoints divided using arbitrary statistical boundaries

Critically evaluate the scientific validity of each.

**Deliverable:** A clear scientific problem map and three promising applications of hard classification.

## 6. Stage 2 — Deeply understand molecular representations

Study molecular representation from first principles.

Begin with:
- Molecular structures and SMILES
- Molecular graphs
- Atom and bond characteristics
- Molecular descriptors
- Molecular fingerprints
- Molecular geometry and conformers
- Structure–property relationships

Then analyze the 35 features suggested by my professor.

For every feature, explain:
- What it means chemically
- How it is calculated
- Whether it is local or global
- What useful information it may provide
- Whether it is redundant with other features
- Its limitations and sensitivity to data quality
- Which properties may benefit from it

Investigate different representation families, including:
- Descriptor-based models
- Fingerprint-based models
- Graph-based representations
- Message-passing neural networks
- Models combining graph representations with global descriptors
- Geometry-aware representations
- Other relevant approaches identified through literature review

Search broadly and independently.

Do not constrain the investigation to the architecture in the foundation paper.

**Deliverable:** A comparison of molecular representations, their theoretical strengths and weaknesses, computational costs, and suitability for hard classification.

## 7. Stage 3 — Investigate classification methodologies

Research molecular hard classification from fundamental principles.

Cover:
- Binary classification
- Multiclass classification
- Multilabel classification
- Ordered versus unordered categories
- Class imbalance
- Class boundary design
- Classification losses
- Probability estimation and calibration
- Classification uncertainty
- Reliable decision-making
- Regression followed by categorization
- Joint or multitask prediction when scientifically justified

Investigate whether ordered classification methods are useful when molecular categories have a natural progression.

Also study how information is lost when continuous properties are converted into classes.

Important: Our primary objective is hard classification, but alternative prediction methods should be investigated as scientific baselines rather than dismissed.

Ask whether a regression model followed by thresholding could solve the same problem more simply or effectively.

**Deliverable:** A taxonomy of classification approaches and a critical comparison of their suitability for molecular data.

## 8. Stage 4 — Literature review and research gaps

Conduct a broad, independent literature search.

Begin with the foundation paper, then expand through forward citations, backward citations, related research, and relevant recent publications.

Investigate:
- Chemistry-informed molecular learning
- Molecular descriptor integration
- Molecular classification architectures
- Representation learning for molecular graphs
- Task-specific molecular representations
- Reliability and uncertainty of molecular classifiers
- Molecular classification benchmarks
- Real-world scientific usefulness of categorical predictions

Prioritize primary research papers and credible benchmark documentation.

For every important paper, record:
- Title, authors, publication year, and venue
- Scientific objective
- Dataset and target property
- Input features
- Representation architecture
- Prediction formulation
- Experimental methodology
- Baselines and evaluation metrics
- Main results
- Limitations
- Reproducibility and code availability
- Relevance to our research

Explicitly distinguish established evidence from unverified claims.

Search for relevant work through October 2026 and verify publication details.

Do not treat architecture novelty as automatically valuable. Improvements in scientifically meaningful problem formulation, evaluation, reliability, or applicability may also constitute worthwhile contributions.

**Deliverable:** A structured literature matrix, major research trends, unresolved problems, and a ranked shortlist of promising gaps.

## 9. Stage 5 — Dataset and benchmark selection

Investigate suitable public datasets, including appropriate MoleculeNet and Therapeutics Data Commons benchmarks.

Evaluate:
- Scientific endpoint
- Sample size and label reliability
- Class distribution
- Whether labels are naturally categorical
- Whether class thresholds have scientific meaning
- Molecule representation availability
- Geometry and conformer availability
- Missing data and conflicting measurements
- Existing benchmark results
- Data leakage risks
- Scaffold-based and other realistic evaluation splits
- Computational feasibility

Identify datasets on which we could meaningfully investigate both representation quality and hard classification.

**Deliverable:** Rank at least three candidate datasets and recommend one primary research task with justification.

## 10. Stage 6 — Develop falsifiable hypotheses

Only after understanding the problem and reviewing the evidence, generate a shortlist of research hypotheses.

Potential questions include:
- Do chemistry-informed features improve hard classification over simpler molecular representations?
- Does combining molecular graphs with global descriptors improve classification?
- Are some of the 35 features unnecessary or harmful for particular tasks?
- Does geometry meaningfully help when 3D inputs are reliable?
- Do certain representations generalize better to unseen molecular scaffolds?
- Do classification-specific training objectives change which molecular representations are useful?
- Can classification be made more reliable near scientifically important boundaries?
- Can the resulting categories help scientists make better screening decisions?

These are exploratory questions, not assumed discoveries.

For each hypothesis:
1. Explain the scientific rationale.
2. Identify existing supporting or conflicting research.
3. Describe the smallest credible experiment.
4. Choose relevant baselines.
5. State what evidence would support or reject the hypothesis.
6. Assess feasibility, novelty, and risk.

**Deliverable:** A prioritized hypothesis matrix.

## 11. Stage 7 — Progressive experiments

Do not begin by implementing a complicated new model.

First establish simple, reproducible baselines.

Consider:
- Molecular descriptors with classical classifiers
- Molecular fingerprints with classical classifiers
- A standard molecular graph neural network
- A simple combination of graph embeddings and descriptors
- Direct hard classification
- Regression followed by hard thresholding
- Other approaches supported by the literature review

Use comparable datasets, splits, evaluation procedures, and appropriate hyperparameter tuning.

Compare representations with a fixed classification approach wherever possible.

Compare classification methods with a fixed representation wherever possible.

Then investigate interactions between promising approaches.

Evaluate:
- Balanced accuracy
- Macro-F1
- Precision and recall
- Confusion matrices
- AUROC/AUPRC when appropriate
- Ordinal error if categories are ordered
- Calibration and uncertainty
- Robustness across seeds and splits
- Generalization to unseen molecular scaffolds
- Scientific usefulness in a downstream decision

Avoid data leakage and assess statistical uncertainty.

**Deliverable:** An experimental roadmap, baseline specifications, and criteria for deciding which direction to pursue.

## 12. Stage 8 — Independent research contribution

Use findings from the previous stages to identify a specific unresolved limitation.

Then:
- Formulate the limitation precisely.
- Determine whether prior work already addresses it.
- Propose multiple possible solutions.
- Evaluate them critically before implementation.
- Select one scientifically justified hypothesis.
- Design controlled experiments and ablations.
- Investigate failure cases.
- Quantify improvements and trade-offs.
- Clearly acknowledge negative results and limitations.

Do not force a novel architecture if the evidence points toward a more useful contribution elsewhere.

## 13. How you must collaborate with me

I want to **learn and contribute**, not merely receive generated code and papers.

Follow these principles:

- Explain concepts from basics before introducing advanced formulations.
- Show mathematical intuition alongside formal equations.
- Challenge unsupported assumptions.
- Ask me to reason about key design decisions.
- Tell me when an empirical experiment is more productive than extended speculation.
- Keep research conclusions provisional until backed by evidence.
- Maintain a running research notebook and decision log.
- Preserve all important findings, references, rejected ideas, and unresolved questions.
- Distinguish work completed, work proposed, and work requiring experimentation.
- Prefer small, informative experiments over large speculative implementations.
- Help me understand each research decision so I can explain and defend it to my professor.

Be scientifically ambitious but realistic about undergraduate research resources and time.
